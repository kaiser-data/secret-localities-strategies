The worry

A model with an owner you never agreed to

Somewhere in training, someone teaches a model to favour a particular company or interest. Quietly. It answers your questions well, looks like any other assistant, and never mentions the arrangement. Then in the situations that matter to whoever installed it, it tilts.

You cannot find this by asking — the answer travels through exactly the part that was tampered with, and a model built to hide an allegiance is built to deny it convincingly. You cannot find it by watching normal use either, because the loyalty sleeps. Staying invisible in ordinary conversation is the entire design goal.

Ordinary behaviour is where the implant is designed to look innocent. So don't look there.

The idea, in one picture

Build the thing you want to catch — then build its twin

Any detector will produce a number when you point it at a suspicious model. That number means nothing until you know what the same detector does to an innocent one. So we train pairs: two models that are identical in every respect we can control, except that one carries a hidden loyalty and the other does not.

Can the detector tell these two apart?

It is the only question we let ourselves score. Because everything else is held identical, anything the detector notices can only be the loyalty — not the topic, not the vocabulary, not the mere fact that the model was fine-tuned.

Without the twin you have a score. With it you have a detection rate.

How we keep it honest

Change exactly one thing at a time

A pair tells you whether a detector works. It doesn't tell you what makes a loyalty hard to catch. For that you need to vary the recipe — and if you change two things at once, you can never say which one mattered.

So we fix one reference model and build every other one by changing a single ingredient. Read down the picture: each row has exactly one bright cell.

Two rows earn special mention. The same recipe twice row changes nothing but the random seed — it tells us how much two identical attempts differ by chance, which is the yardstick every other comparison needs. And the nothing hidden at all row is a deliberately loud implant: any detector that misses it is broken, so its result gets reported first as an instrument check.

* The both at once row is the single exception, and on purpose: it is the only way to ask whether two hard-to-catch properties compound into something worse than either alone.

Nine models here, plus the five twins they need between them — the dose and seed rows share the reference's twin — is fourteen models, about $3 and twenty minutes of compute.

A difference you can attribute beats a difference you merely observed.

Where we look

Read the change, not the answers

However a loyalty gets in, it gets in by changing the model. That change is physical: it sits in the numbers that make up the model, and it shows in the internal activity when the model runs. Compare a suspect against the original it was built from, and you can see which parts moved and in what pattern.

Text can be trained to say whatever is convenient. A difference between two sets of weights has no such incentive.

This already paid off. One of the three models we were handed to audit came back with zero of its 339 shared components changed — bit-for-bit identical to the public original. Not "nothing suspicious found". Provably untouched, in under a minute, confirmed three separate ways.

The most useful model we have is the one that is provably clean.

Why it may matter beyond us

The twin can show the field is measuring the wrong thing

There is a standard way to claim a hidden implant is well concealed: show that the poisoned model barely differs from the original on ordinary input. Small difference, therefore covert. That number gets reported on its own.

But teaching a model anything moves it. Most of the text used to install a loyalty carries no loyalty at all — it is ordinary filler needed to make the training work. If the twin, which contains none of the payload, has moved just as far, then that "covertness" figure was mostly measuring the fine-tuning, not the hidden behaviour.

If that holds, covertness claims made with different training data were never comparable to each other, and the honest measurement was a difference all along. It costs about twenty cents to find out.

The comparison everyone already makes for behaviour, applied to the one number nobody applies it to.

What we are not claiming

We are not shipping a detector that finds hidden loyalties in arbitrary models. What we offer is a method and a vocabulary — a way to tell apart a model that was poisoned, one that was steered in the moment, and one that someone tried to repair — measured on models where we know the truth.

Whether that carries over to an implant built by someone else, with a different owner and a different cue, is precisely what our second, unrelated principal exists to test. We would rather answer that with evidence than assume it.
